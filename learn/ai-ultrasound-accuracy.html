How accurate is AI at reading ultrasounds?

What accuracy numbers really mean, using Cathra’s own test results as a worked example.

From Cathra · Updated November 25, 2026 · General education, not medical advice

Short answer

It depends on the tool, the task, and especially how it was tested. A single accuracy number can look impressive while hiding serious weaknesses, so it helps to know a few other measures and to ask where the test images came from.

Why one number can mislead

Cathra’s first model (v1) scored 74.2% accuracy on 1,449 test images. That sounds decent, but it correctly recognized only 19.2% of normal images. Because most test images were abnormal, a model that called almost everything abnormal still looked fairly accurate.

Cathra’s current model (v2) was trained on balanced data. It scored 85.6% accuracy and, more importantly, recognized 70.8% of normal images and 91.4% of abnormal ones.

The measures that matter

  • Sensitivity: of the truly abnormal images, how many the AI called abnormal.
  • Specificity: of the truly normal images, how many the AI called normal.
  • Balanced accuracy: the average of the two, so a lopsided test set can’t hide a weak spot. Cathra v1 scored 57.6% here; v2 scored 81.1%.
  • Positive predictive value: when the AI says “abnormal,” how often it is right. This depends heavily on how common the condition is in the group being tested.

Where the test images came from matters most

  • Same source vs. new sources: models are often tested on images from the same dataset they learned from. Results on images from other hospitals, machines, or patients are usually lower.
  • Near-duplicate images: if test images are near-copies of training images, results look better than they really are. Learn why.
  • Confidence: a good tool should say when it isn’t sure. Cathra only names a pattern when its scores clear calibrated thresholds, and otherwise says it can’t tell.

Questions to ask about any ultrasound AI

  • What were sensitivity and specificity, not just accuracy?
  • Was it tested on images from different hospitals or machines?
  • Were duplicate or near-duplicate images removed before testing?
  • Has it been reviewed or cleared by a regulator for clinical use?

Cathra is a research and education tool. It has not been clinically validated or cleared by any regulator, and its results come from one public dataset.
