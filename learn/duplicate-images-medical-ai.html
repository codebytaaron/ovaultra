Why duplicate images make medical AI look better than it is

How copied and mislabeled images quietly inflate results, and how to check a dataset for free.

From Cathra · Updated November 25, 2026 · General education, not medical advice

Short answer

When the same or nearly the same image appears in both the training set and the test set, an AI model is partly being tested on images it has already seen. That makes its accuracy look higher than it would be on truly new images. When the same image appears with two different labels, no model can get both right.

How duplicates sneak in

  • Video frames: ultrasound datasets often include several frames from the same scan, which look almost identical.
  • Re-saved or resized copies: the same picture saved at a different size or quality.
  • Images collected from the web: the same image can be downloaded from several places.

A real example

Running Cathra Dataset Check on the public PCOSGen ovarian ultrasound dataset (4,569 images after removing exact duplicate files), at its recommended setting, flagged:

  • 3,626 images (79%) that have at least one near-copy in the dataset,
  • 5,173 near-copy pairs that cross the training and test sets, and
  • 7,939 near-copy pairs where the two images carry different labels.

Looking similar isn’t always the same as being a true copy, so matches should be reviewed by hand. Still, numbers like these mean test results on this dataset, including Cathra’s own, may look better than they would on images from new sources. Cathra says so on its How it works page.

How to avoid it

  • Remove exact and near-duplicate images before splitting data into training and test sets.
  • Split by patient or by scan, not by individual image, so frames from one scan stay together.
  • Review images that appear with conflicting labels.
  • Report results on a cleaned test set, and say what was removed.
