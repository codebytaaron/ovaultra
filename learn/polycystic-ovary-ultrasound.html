What does a polycystic ovary look like on ultrasound?

What “polycystic ovarian morphology” means, how it is measured, and how it relates to PCOS.

From Cathra · Updated November 25, 2026 · General education, not medical advice

Short answer

A polycystic ovary usually looks larger than average and contains many small, dark, round follicles, often arranged around its outer edge. Under the 2023 international PCOS guideline, doctors call this polycystic ovarian morphology (PCOM) in adults when one ovary has 20 or more follicles, or an ovarian volume of 10 mL or more.

What doctors look for

  • Many small follicles: numerous small, dark, fluid-filled follicles in one or both ovaries, often lined up around the edge.
  • Follicle count: 20 or more follicles in at least one ovary, counted on a transvaginal scan with a modern, high-frequency probe.
  • Ovarian volume: 10 mL or more in at least one ovary. Volume is used on its own when image quality or older equipment makes counting follicles unreliable.
  • These measurements are taken when there is no dominant follicle, corpus luteum, or cyst that would change them.

Polycystic ovaries are not the same as PCOS

The name is confusing. Polycystic ovarian morphology describes how an ovary looks. Polycystic ovary syndrome (PCOS) is a hormonal condition. In adults, PCOS is diagnosed when at least two of these three are present, after other causes have been ruled out:

  • irregular periods or ovulation problems,
  • signs of high androgens (“male” hormones), seen in symptoms or blood tests,
  • polycystic ovarian morphology on ultrasound, or a high AMH blood level.

That means someone can have polycystic-looking ovaries without PCOS, and someone can have PCOS with normal-looking ovaries.

Teens are different

Many teenagers naturally have ovaries with lots of follicles. The guideline says ultrasound should not be used to diagnose PCOS in adolescents, generally within 8 years of their first period.
