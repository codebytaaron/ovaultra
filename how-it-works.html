How Cathra works

What Cathra was trained on, what its scores mean, and where it falls short.

What it’s for

You give Cathra an existing ovarian ultrasound image. It compares the image to two visual patterns it learned during training, normal ovarian appearance and abnormal ovarian appearance, and returns a similarity score for each. Neither result confirms or rules out cancer or any other condition.

Training data

The current model (v2) was trained on 1,734 labeled ovarian ultrasound images, 867 in each category, and tested on a separate set of 1,449 images it never saw during training. Exact duplicate files were removed. Some test images are near-copies of training images, such as frames from the same scan, which can make test results look better than they are.

  • Normal: ovaries with a typical appearance.
  • Abnormal: ovaries that differ from a typical appearance. In this dataset, most abnormal images show a polycystic ovarian appearance.

Dataset credit

Training images come from the PCOSGen dataset (train and test sets), created for the Auto-PCOS classification challenge by Palak Handa, Anushka Saini, Siddhant Dutta, Harsh Pathak, Nishi Choudhary, Nidhi Goel, and Jasdeep Kaur Dhanao. Published on Zenodo, 2025: train · test. Used under the CC BY 4.0 license. Images were sorted into Normal and Abnormal using the dataset’s own labels.

Test results

I tested both versions of the model on the same 1,449 PCOSGen test images (411 normal, 1,038 abnormal), none of which were used in training. Each image was scored by the same model the website uses, counting the higher of its two scores as the answer.

v1 (July 2026)v2 (November 2026)
Overall accuracy74.2%85.6%
Balanced accuracy57.6%81.1%
Normal images matched correctly19.2% (79 of 411)70.8% (291 of 411)
Abnormal images matched correctly96.0% (996 of 1,038)91.4% (949 of 1,038)
  • What improved: v2 recognizes normal images far more reliably, rising from 19% to 71%, so its results are much more balanced across both patterns.
  • What changed slightly: v2 matches 91% of abnormal images, compared with 96% for v1.
  • Balanced accuracy gives equal weight to normal and abnormal images. I report it because most of the test images are abnormal.
  • Context: v2 was trained and tested on images from the same dataset. Results on images from other sources may differ.

Full methods and results are in the Cathra paper (PDF). These are research results on a public image dataset. They are not clinical performance figures and do not describe how Cathra would perform on any individual’s scan.

Reading the results

Each percentage is a visual similarity score: how closely the image resembles that pattern. It is not the probability that a person has any condition.

Cathra names a pattern only when it is confident enough. Its answer rule was calibrated with conformal prediction, a statistical method that sets thresholds from test data so that errors stay below a chosen rate. I targeted at most about 1 in 10 wrong answers for each pattern.

  • Normal pattern: the normal score is 60% or higher.
  • Abnormal pattern: the abnormal score is about 97% or higher.
  • Anything in between: “Unable to assess confidently.”

The abnormal bar is higher because the model tends to over-call abnormal. On test images held out from calibration, Cathra gave an answer for about 9 in 10 images, and about 90% of those answers matched the dataset’s label. Normal images wrongly labeled abnormal fell from 28% to about 10%.

This guarantee only applies to images similar to the PCOSGen test set. It says nothing about images from other sources, and it does not make the scores a diagnosis.

Questions

Is this a diagnosis?

No. Cathra is a research and education tool. It shows how closely an image matches two visual patterns it learned. It does not diagnose, detect, confirm, or rule out any condition. Only a qualified medical professional can interpret an ultrasound.

Is my image stored?

No. Your image is analyzed on your own device and is never uploaded, saved, or sent anywhere. When you choose another image or close the page, it is discarded.

What images work?

A single ovarian ultrasound view, saved as a JPG or PNG. Grayscale and Doppler scans both work. Higher-resolution images with fewer on-screen labels work best. Avoid collages, photos of screens, and screenshots of whole web pages. Cathra analyzes the center square of the image, which it outlines before you analyze.

Does it detect PCOS?

No. Cathra compares an image to normal and abnormal ovarian appearance. In its training data, most abnormal images show a polycystic appearance, but PCOS is diagnosed by a doctor using several criteria, not a single image.

Why does it say “Unable to assess confidently”?

Cathra only names a pattern when its scores clear a calibrated threshold: 60% or higher for normal, and about 97% or higher for abnormal. When the scores fall in between, it says it can’t tell instead of guessing. See Reading the results.

Limitations

  • Cathra is a research tool and has not been clinically validated. Like any model, it can be wrong (see test results).
  • Its training images come from one public research dataset gathered from online sources. Performance on images from other hospitals, machines, or patient groups hasn’t been tested.
  • Image quality, cropping, on-screen labels, and annotations can affect the scores.
  • It compares two broad patterns. It doesn’t identify specific conditions or assign clinical categories such as O-RADS.
  • It’s designed only for ovarian ultrasound images. Other images can still produce scores, but those scores have no meaning.
