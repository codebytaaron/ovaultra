How Cathra works

What it was trained on, what its scores mean, and where it falls short.

What it does

You give Cathra an existing ovarian ultrasound image. It compares the image to two visual patterns it learned during training, normal ovarian appearance and abnormal ovarian appearance, and returns a similarity score for each. It does not diagnose anything.

Training data

The current model (v2) was trained on 1,734 labeled ovarian ultrasound images, 867 in each category, and tested on a separate set of 1,449 images it never saw during training. Exact duplicates were removed, and no test image is a copy of a training image.

  • Normal: ovaries with a typical appearance.
  • Abnormal: a broad category of non-cancerous findings. In this dataset, most abnormal images show a polycystic ovarian appearance. An abnormal-pattern result does not mean cancer.

Dataset credit

Training images come from the PCOSGen dataset (train and test sets), created for the Auto-PCOS classification challenge by Palak Handa, Anushka Saini, Siddhant Dutta, Harsh Pathak, Nishi Choudhary, Nidhi Goel, and Jasdeep Kaur Dhanao. Published on Zenodo, 2025: train · test. Used under the CC BY 4.0 license. Images were sorted into Normal and Abnormal using the dataset’s own labels.

How accurate is it?

Both versions of the model were tested on the same 1,449 held-out PCOSGen test images (411 normal, 1,038 abnormal). Each image was scored exactly as the website scores it.

v1 (July 2026)v2 (November 2026)
Overall accuracy74.2%85.6%
Balanced accuracy57.6%81.1%
Normal scans called normal19.2% (79 of 411)70.8% (291 of 411)
Abnormal scans called abnormal96.0% (996 of 1,038)91.4% (949 of 1,038)
  • Why v2 is better: v1 labeled almost every image abnormal, so it recognized only 1 in 5 normal scans. v2 actually tells the two patterns apart.
  • The trade-off: v2 catches slightly fewer abnormal scans than v1 (91.4% vs 96.0%).
  • Balanced accuracy averages the two rates, because the test set is mostly abnormal and overall accuracy alone can hide a model that favors one answer.
  • v1 was trained on a smaller set of images whose source isn't documented. v2's training and test images come from the same source, which may make its score higher than it would be on images from other hospitals or machines.

Reading the results

  • Each percentage is a visual similarity score: how closely the image resembles that pattern. It is not the probability that a person has any condition.
  • The higher of the two scores is shown as the result label.
  • If the higher score is below 60%, or the two scores are within 15 points of each other, Cathra shows “Unable to assess confidently” instead of picking one.

Limitations

  • It has not been clinically validated. On the test set it misses about 1 in 11 abnormal scans and calls about 3 in 10 normal scans abnormal.
  • It was trained and tested on one dataset of images collected from the internet. Results on images from other hospitals, ultrasound machines, or patient populations are unknown.
  • Image quality, cropping, on-screen labels, and annotations can change the scores.
  • It only knows two broad categories. It cannot identify specific conditions or assign clinical categories such as O-RADS.
  • It will still return scores for images that are not ovarian ultrasounds.
